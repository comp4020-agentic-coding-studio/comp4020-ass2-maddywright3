Nobody chooses a restaurant because of its bathroom. A global survey of
more than 4,000 diners found that 100% agreed a dirty restroom reflects
badly on the establishment — and 80% said they would not, or might not,
return to a restaurant after finding one. Read those two numbers
together and you get the actual shape of the problem: the bathroom
contributes nothing to the decision that gets someone through the door,
and almost everything to the decision about whether they ever come
back. It's a cost centre with no matching revenue line, which is exactly
why it's the first thing an under-pressure manager lets slide.

This is a different mechanism again from the last three weeks, and worth
being precise about. Software's last mile is expensive because of
compounding debt. Broadband's is expensive because of geometry. A
restaurant bathroom's last mile is expensive because the cost of
neglecting it is invisible at the point of neglect — nobody complains on
the way out, they just don't come back, and by the time that shows up in
the numbers it's aggregated into "quiet slump," not "the bathroom."
Compare that to a badly plated dish: a customer sends it back
immediately, the kitchen hears about it that night. The last mile here
is expensive because the feedback loop that would normally force a fix
is broken.

That's the complication worth extending the thesis with: not every
last-mile cost is hidden because someone is avoiding it, the way a
software team avoids hardening or a PhD student avoids writing. Some are
hidden because the business genuinely cannot see the bill arriving —
the restroom doesn't generate a complaint, a review, or a refund
request; it generates an absence, and absences don't file tickets. A
manager working entirely from complaint volume will rank the bathroom as
fine, right up until repeat-visit numbers quietly erode for reasons
nobody has a report for. The fix isn't willpower, it's measurement: you
have to go looking for the cost that the ordinary feedback channel was
never built to surface.
