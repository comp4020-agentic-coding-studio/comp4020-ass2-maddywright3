Richard Gabriel's 1989 essay "The Rise of 'Worse Is Better'" is the one
genuine counter-example this course needs to take seriously, because
it's an argument from someone who built the "right thing" and watched it
lose anyway. Gabriel contrasted two design philosophies: "the right
thing," which prizes correctness, consistency, and completeness above
all — his own example is Common Lisp — against "worse is better," which
accepts a simpler, less complete implementation because simplicity wins
adoption. His conclusion, stated with visible discomfort at his own
argument: worse-is-better software "has better survival characteristics
than the-right-thing," and unfinished-on-purpose, simple tools
repeatedly out-competed more complete, more correct ones — C over Lisp,
Unix over Lisp machines — because by the time the complete version was
actually finished, the market had already adopted the incomplete one
and couldn't switch back.

Every earlier week in this course argued that stopping short costs you
later. This week argues the opposite is also true, and that's the
specific complication worth taking as seriously as the thesis itself:
continued polish doesn't just cost more per increment, past some point
it actively subtracts from what users wanted, because every additional
feature is also additional surface area — more to load, more to learn,
more ways for the simple thing someone chose to stop being simple.

This is where the thesis has to be complicated, not just extended. "The
last mile is expensive, so budget for it" is good advice up to the point
where the thing being finished has already delivered what its users
came for. Past that point, the same finishing instinct that fixed
software's shortcuts in week one starts actively working against the
product: complexity that has to be maintained, learned, and shipped
around forever, in exchange for value that's gone flat or turned
negative. Gabriel's own ambivalence about his essay — he called it "an
intentional caricature" even while conceding it kept being true — is
the right note to end this week on. The thesis "finish the thing" is
correct far more often than it's wrong. This is the week that shows
where it stops being correct, and a course that never admitted that
would be worse evidence for its own argument, not better.
