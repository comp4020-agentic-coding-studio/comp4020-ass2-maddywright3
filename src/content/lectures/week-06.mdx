Every other week in this course has had to argue its way to "the last
mile is disproportionately expensive." Manufacturing reaches for the
accounting instead — quality engineers classify cost into prevention,
appraisal, and failure, and the specific multiplier chart everyone
reaches for (1x at design, 6.5x at implementation, 15x at testing, 60
to 100x after release) gets credited to something called the "IBM
Systems Sciences Institute." Here's the complication worth admitting
before leaning on it: that institute's own underlying study has never
been produced. Software researchers Hillel Wayne and Laurent Bossavit
traced the citation trail back through decades of textbooks and found
it dead-ends in 1981 internal IBM training notes with no public dataset
behind them — a number that got laundered into "settled research" by
being repeated, not by being re-measured. A course built on finishing
things properly shouldn't cite a stat that never finished being
checked.

What survives that scrutiny is smaller but real: Boehm and Basili's
peer-reviewed 2001 IEEE Computer paper found that fixing a defect after
delivery runs roughly 100 times more expensive than catching it during
requirements and design on large projects — while adding their own
caveat that the ratio drops to closer to 5:1 on small, non-critical
ones. The order of magnitude survived independent replication. The
specific chart with the impressive-sounding institute's name attached
to it did not.

The reason this multiplier exists is the same reason software's last
mile compounds, but manufacturing makes it literal instead of
architectural: every stage a defect survives, it gets buried under more
finished work built on top of it. A flawed part caught on the design
bench is a sketch you redraw. The identical flaw caught in a shipped
product is a recall, a warranty claim, a support call, a reputational
hit, and — if it's mechanical rather than digital — physical units
already sitting in warehouses and driveways that all need to be found
and touched again.

That slider is deliberately unit-less, and it's worth saying why rather than
quietly picking a currency. Every dollar-denominated version of this
multiplier we could find traces back to the same IBM training-note lineage
already flagged above as unverifiable — putting a dollar sign on it would
just launder the number a second time. Boehm and Basili's own replicated
figure isn't costed either: they measured a ratio of relative effort, not a
price. NIST's 2002 report does contain a real, defensible dollar figure —
software defects were estimated to cost the US economy $59.5 billion a
year, with $22.2 billion of that recoverable through better testing
infrastructure — but that's a national, economy-wide aggregate, not a
per-defect or per-phase cost, so it can't be repurposed to build a curve
like this one without inventing the missing step ourselves. So the honest
choice is the unit-less one: the slider reads in relative cost, stated
plainly as an index rather than dressed up as a figure it isn't.

Here's the complication worth sitting with: even the honest, surviving
version of this multiplier is exactly why "finish inspection later" is
never a rational cost-saving move in manufacturing, even though it
looks like one on this quarter's budget. Skipping appraisal costs early
doesn't remove the cost of the defects that were there anyway — it just
relocates that cost to the far end of the curve, where the
peer-reviewed order of magnitude is still roughly a hundred, and now
includes customers. The thesis usually shows up as "finishing was more
expensive than expected." Manufacturing's version is sharper: finishing
wasn't more expensive than expected, it was exactly as expensive as
Boehm and Basili's replicated finding said it would be — the surprise
was only in not multiplying it out in advance, and in how long a made-up
institute's name got away with standing in for that real result.
