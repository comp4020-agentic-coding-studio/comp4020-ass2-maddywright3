Building departments are specific about what "almost complete" means:
functionally complete, with only minor items remaining, not 85% or even
95%. A developer who requests final inspection while real work remains
routinely gets refused, and that refusal isn't paperwork friction — it's
the entire point of the last mile in this domain. A punch list is
normally created at 95–98% completion and should, on paper, take a
few weeks to close out. But retainage — most contracts hold back 5–10%
of total payment until every punch-list item is done — means the
contractor's own cash is now locked up specifically by the smallest
items on the list. And every day the certificate of occupancy is
delayed is a day of rent, sale proceeds, or lease income the building is
legally barred from earning, on top of possible liquidated damages and
tenants who can walk away from a lease entirely if a promised move-in
date slips.

Notice what the curve does at the very end: it doesn't keep climbing
to 100%, it drops. That's the domain-specific complication worth adding
to the thesis. In software, the last mile is expensive continuously —
hardening never has a single moment where the cost resets. In
construction, the cost is a spike that resolves the instant the
certificate is actually issued, because occupancy is a threshold, not a
gradient: the building goes from "cannot legally be used" to "can be
used" in one inspection, and every dollar of delay cost stops accruing
the moment that gate opens. This is a genuinely different failure shape
from anything earlier in the course — not a debt that compounds forever,
but a toll that's brutal right up to a fixed gate and vanishes the
instant that gate opens. The lesson for anyone managing a last mile like
this isn't "finish faster," it's "identify exactly where your gate is,"
because the true cost curve is invisible if you're only tracking percent
complete instead of distance to the actual legal threshold.
