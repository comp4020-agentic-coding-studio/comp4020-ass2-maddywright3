Boeing has published a Statistical Summary of Commercial Jet Airplane
Accidents every year for decades, and the pattern in it has barely
moved across editions. Across the 2004–2013 decade it covers, 22% of
fatal accidents happened during final approach and 25% during landing —
47% of all fatal accidents, and roughly 40% of onboard fatalities,
concentrated into the two phases that, combined, last only a few minutes
out of a multi-hour flight. Cruise, which is most of a flight's actual
duration, accounted for only about 10% of fatal accidents. This isn't a
one-off dataset either — the same concentration shows up in Boeing's
1999–2008, 2009–2018, and 2013–2022 editions alike.

That mismatch — minutes of flight time, half the fatal risk — is the
specific, measurable version of this course's thesis that aviation
supplies. It isn't that landing is technically harder than cruising in
some vague sense; it's that landing is the phase where the aircraft is
closest to the ground, closest to other traffic, and has the least room
to recover from an error, all at once, right at the point where the
flight is otherwise "basically done."

The complication for the thesis: aviation's whole safety culture is
built around treating the last mile as categorically different, not
just "a bit riskier," which is a stronger response than any other domain
in this course has taken. Stabilised-approach criteria, go-around
procedures, and mandatory landing checklists all exist specifically to
make the final phase harder to rush, precisely because the industry's
own long-run data says rushing it is where the fatal accidents
concentrate. That's the opposite instinct from software's "ship the
demo, harden later" or a startup's "hit this quarter's number" — aviation
treats its last mile as the part you're least allowed to treat casually,
not the part you're most tempted to. Every other domain in this course
has had to learn that lesson the hard way, from a failure. Aviation
built its entire procedural culture around already knowing it.
