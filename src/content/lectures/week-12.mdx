Eleven weeks, eleven domains, and — if this course did its job — eleven
different answers to "why is the last mile expensive here," not one
answer repeated eleven times with a new coat of paint. Software's last
mile was compounding technical debt. Broadband's was geometry nobody
chose. A PhD's was a missing external deadline. A restaurant bathroom's
was a broken feedback loop. Law's was an irreversible step function.
Manufacturing's was a literal, measured multiplier. A cross-team
handover's was a seam between two finished things. A startup's was a
target that moves. Construction's was a legal threshold, not a slope.
Aviation's was a few minutes carrying half the risk. Open-source
overbuilding's was the thesis running past its own limit into negative
value. If there's one thing all eleven share, it isn't a shape of curve
— it's a blind spot: in every domain, the people closest to the work
kept underestimating the specific cost of their own last mile, usually
because that cost wasn't the kind their normal tracking was built to
see.

There's a fitting, if contested, psychological frame for why "finish"
sticks with us the way it does. Bluma Zeigarnik's 1927 studies found
that people recalled interrupted, unfinished tasks at roughly twice the
rate of completed ones — the unresolved task keeps generating mental
tension that a finished one doesn't. Worth saying plainly: later
replications have been inconsistent, and a 2025 meta-analysis found no
reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks at all, only a general
tendency to want to resume them. That mixed, argued-over status is
actually the right closing image for this course rather than a
liability — it's a hundred years of psychologists disagreeing about
exactly how much unfinished work costs us psychologically, which is the
same argument this course has been having about how much it costs
practically, domain by domain, for twelve weeks.
