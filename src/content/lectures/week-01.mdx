A demo and a shipped product are not the same artefact at 90% and 100%
completion. They are two different projects that happen to share a
codebase. The demo's job is to convince someone the idea works; the
shipped product's job is to keep working when someone you've never met,
on a network you don't control, does something you didn't anticipate. You
can get most of the way to the second by doing more of what got you the
first — more features, more polish — and none of it touches the part that
was actually missing.

Here's the specific thing that makes this expensive, not just slow: every
shortcut you took to reach the demo is a decision baked into the
architecture, not a checkbox you tick later. No auth boundary because it
was just you testing. No input validation because you control the input.
No retry logic because the network never dropped in your office. Every
one of those is fine in isolation and free at demo time. But they don't
sit next to each other quietly — they interact. Skip auth *and* skip rate
limiting and you don't have two small gaps, you have an open door with no
lock and no camera. Fixing one exposes how much the design assumed the
other was also missing. That's why "the last 10%" of a software project
routinely costs as much as the first 90%: it isn't 10% more feature work,
it's the bill for every assumption the first 90% quietly made.

This is where the ninety-ninety rule comes from — not as a joke about bad
estimating, but as an accurate description of where the actual unknowns
live. The features are knowable in advance. The corners cut to reach them
are not enumerated anywhere, and you only discover the full set of them
by trying to make the thing survive contact with someone who isn't you.

Duke Nukem Forever is the extreme case, not the exception that proves the
rule wrong. It spent fourteen years in development, was rebooted six
times, and became a running joke — "Duke Nukem Never" — precisely because
its studio kept doing more of the first kind of work (new engines, new
set pieces, whatever impressed the team that week) instead of closing out
the second kind. Feature creep isn't a distraction from finishing; in
software specifically, it's a way of avoiding it, because adding a new
feature feels like progress and paying down the interest on old shortcuts
does not.

The complication worth sitting with: this means the cost of finishing
isn't fixed by how much is left to build. It's fixed by how many
load-bearing shortcuts you took to get here, and those compound with each
other rather than adding up — which is why teams that feel "90% done" for
months aren't lying to themselves about the wrong number, they're lying
to themselves about the wrong kind of number entirely.
