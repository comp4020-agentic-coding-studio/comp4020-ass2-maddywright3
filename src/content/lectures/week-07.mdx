NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter is the cleanest case study this course has,
because both halves of the handover it failed at were, individually,
finished correctly. Lockheed Martin built the spacecraft and delivered
thruster-force data exactly as their process specified. JPL's navigation
team wrote trajectory software and fed that data in exactly as their
process specified. The catch: Lockheed's numbers were in pound-seconds of
force, and JPL's model expected newton-seconds — a factor of roughly
4.45 that nobody on either finished team caught, because catching it was
nobody's explicit job. The $125 million spacecraft flew 57 kilometres
above Mars instead of 140, hit atmosphere it was never built to survive,
and broke apart.

That's the specific, expensive thing about this domain's last mile:
unlike a solo software team's shortcuts, or a construction site's punch
list, the failure here doesn't live inside either team's finished work —
it lives in the boundary between them, where a document was traded for
knowledge that never fully fit on the page. Explicit knowledge —
formats, specs, file layouts — survives a handover reliably. Tacit
knowledge — the assumption that "force" obviously means one particular
unit, because that's what everyone in this building has always meant by
it — does not, and nothing in a standard handover checklist is designed
to surface an assumption nobody thought to write down because nobody
thought it was a choice.

The complication this adds to the thesis: "done" can be true twice and
the project can still fail completely. Lockheed finished. JPL finished.
Neither team's retrospective would find anything to fix in their own
process, because there wasn't anything wrong with their own process —
the defect only exists in the space between two teams' definitions of
done, which is precisely the space that neither team's own quality
checks were ever built to look at. NASA's own review called it "an
end-to-end process problem," not a mistake by either team — which is
the whole point. When the last mile is the seam between two finished
things, no amount of finishing either thing better would have caught it;
only someone explicitly responsible for the seam itself could have.
