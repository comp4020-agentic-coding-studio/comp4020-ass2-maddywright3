Lawyers spend years training to argue. Almost none of that training goes
into the thing that actually ends the most careers: the calendar. The most
rigorous answer we have here isn't a rule of thumb, it's a paper: Robert
Jarvis and Debra Moss Vollweiler spent 1,000 hours mining Florida's Office
of Insurance Regulation legal-malpractice database — more than 4,500 claims,
filed against over 100 insurers, across a 44-year window — and found that
missing a statute of limitations or other deadline was the single most
common error behind a malpractice claim: 8.6% of all claims, narrowly ahead
of failing to pursue a claim or defense (8.4%) and negligent drafting
(7.6%). That's a strange place for a profession built on argument to be
losing the most cases: not on the law, but on the calendar.

Here's why this last mile is uniquely unforgiving compared to every
other domain this course has looked at. In software, a shortcut you took
gets more expensive the longer it sits — but it's still, in principle,
fixable. In law, a missed filing deadline is frequently not fixable at
all. Statutes of limitations don't offer a grace period for good
argument; miss the date and the claim is often gone regardless of its
merits. The "cost" of the last mile here isn't a steep number, it's a
step function: negligible right up until the deadline, and then total.

The same dataset also reports what insurers actually paid out, and it's
worth being precise about what that number is and isn't. Across every paid
claim in the sample — not deadline claims specifically, since the study
doesn't break payout size out by cause of claim — insurers paid plaintiffs
an average of $154,139 (in the 70.1% of claims where they paid anything at
all; lawyers themselves paid an average of $11,757 out of pocket, mostly
their deductible). That $154,139 figure is the one real, sourced number the
calculator below is built on, and it anchors the 100%-missed end of the
curve. The shape of the curve leading up to that point isn't a second
measured statistic — no study tracks malpractice exposure at intermediate
points before a deadline is actually blown, because there's nothing to
measure yet. It's the same step-function argument made above, expressed on
the one real dollar figure available rather than an invented one. Treating
that lead-up shape as measured would be exactly the kind of laundered
statistic week 6 spends its own lecture calling out — so it isn't.

The complication this adds to the thesis: not every last mile rewards
"try harder." A firm can be excellent at the law itself — the 90% that
looks like the hard part to an outsider — and still be destroyed by the
10% that looks like paperwork, to the tune of $154,139 on average, and that
number doesn't care how good the underlying argument was. That's precisely
why disciplined firms treat deadline tracking as its own system, separate
from case strategy: dedicated docketing software, redundant calendar
entries, a second person who checks the first person's dates. The lesson
generalizes: when a last-mile failure is irreversible rather than merely
costly, the right response isn't more diligence from the same person who
was already trying — it's redundancy built by design, because a step
function doesn't forgive a single missed step no matter how good
everything before it was.
