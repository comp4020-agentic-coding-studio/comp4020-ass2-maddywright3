Every other week in this course treats "finish" as a fixed target that
gets more expensive to approach. Startup fundraising breaks that model
outright, and CB Insights' own funnel data shows exactly how. Tracking
over 1,100 companies that raised a seed round, only 48% ever raised a
second round at all, and the fraction still standing keeps thinning:
by the fourth round, only 15% of the original group remains. That
attrition isn't explained by companies getting worse. It's explained by
the bar for "good enough to raise" moving. Five years ago, Series A
investors expected under $1 million in annual recurring revenue.
Carta's 2025 data puts the current expectation at $5–10 million — the
same round, a five-to-tenfold higher entry price, without founders
having agreed to anything.

This is the domain that forced this course to build a mechanic none of
the cost-curve weeks needed, because a cost curve says something false
about startups: it implies that closing the distance to the target is
what makes the last mile expensive. Here, closing the distance is what
*moves* the target. A founder who hits this year's traction bar
discovers, on arrival, that the bar was quietly redefined while they
were closing in — not out of malice, but because the entire investor
market recalibrated "enough" upward at the same time, in response to
every other founder also getting better at hitting the old number.

The complication for the thesis is direct: "finishing is a skill you can
get better at" assumes the finish line holds still long enough for
practice to pay off. The Series A crunch shows a case where the finish
line is itself a function of how many people are approaching it — the
2018 seed-to-Series-A graduation rate of roughly 30.6% fell to about
15.4% by 2022, not because founders forgot how to build traction, but
because the definition of sufficient traction is set relative to the
whole cohort's improving performance, not to some fixed bar. Where the
right response to a steep cost curve is "start earlier, plan for the
compounding," the right response to a moving goalpost is different: you
either need a lead large enough to reach a still-future position, or you
need to change the game you're playing (a different investor, a
different milestone, a lower target altogether) rather than trying to
out-run a target that adjusts to your own speed.
