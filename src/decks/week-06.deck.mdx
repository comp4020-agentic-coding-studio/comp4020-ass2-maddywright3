# Cost of quality is a ledger, not a vibe

**Quality engineers already split cost into prevention, appraisal, and
failure — which means manufacturing can put a real number on "the last
mile is expensive" that every other week in this course had to argue its
way toward.**

```notes
This is the week's whole pitch: not a new example of the thesis, an
accounting of it. Say that plainly before the chart goes up, so the room
reads what follows as a ledger entry, not a slogan.
```

---

## The chart everyone in software has seen

Cost to fix a defect, by the stage you catch it at:

| Stage | Multiplier |
| --- | --- |
| Design | 1x |
| Implementation | 6.5x |
| Testing | 15x |
| After release | 60–100x |

Usually credited to something called the **"IBM Systems Sciences Institute."**

```notes
Ask the room: who's seen this chart before, in a lecture or a blog post?
Most hands go up. That's the setup for the next slide.
```

## The problem

> "We'll hone our scholarship skills by looking up the original source for
> these ideas."

**Laurent Bossavit, *The Leprechauns of Software Engineering***

Bossavit devotes a full chapter — "The cost of defects: an illustrated
history" — to tracing this exact chart's citation trail back through
decades of textbooks. It dead-ends in 1981 internal IBM training notes,
with no public dataset behind them.

[Read the chapter yourself →](https://leanpub.com/leprechauns)

```notes
This is the primary source now, not the journalism about it — cite the
book, not just The Register's write-up of the book. A number that got
laundered into "settled research" by being repeated, not by being
re-measured.
```

## What actually survives

**Boehm and Basili, 2001, peer-reviewed, *IEEE Computer*:**

Fixing a defect after delivery runs roughly **100x** more expensive than
catching it at requirements/design — on *large* projects. Their own paper
adds the caveat: closer to **5:1** on small, non-critical ones.

The order of magnitude held up under independent replication. The
impressive-sounding institute's name did not.

---

## Cost of quality, zoomed out

A single defect's multiplier is one line item inside a much bigger number:

- Total quality cost — prevention + appraisal + failure combined — runs
  **10 to 20% of revenue** in a typical, well-run firm.
- In a poorly-run one: **up to 40%**.

That's the American Society for Quality's own benchmark for the ledger
this whole chart lives inside.

## Still true, or just old?

Mike Cohn argues Boehm's curve has been flattening for decades — modern
IDEs, automated testing and CI, and modular architecture each cut into it
before agile even started:

> "The problem isn't that Boehm was wrong. The problem is that we treated
> his findings as if they were timeless."

[Read the argument →](https://mountaingoatsoftware.com/blog/the-cost-of-change-curve-is-outdated)

This is a live disagreement, not a resolved one.

```notes
Don't let this slide land as "so the 100x number is debunked too." It
isn't debunked, it's contested — and presenting it as settled either way
would repeat exactly the mistake this week just spent five slides
examining. If pressed for a verdict: say there isn't one yet, and that's
the honest answer.
```

## The peer-reviewed number, disaggregated

Boehm and Basili didn't publish one multiplier — they published two, and
the gap between them is the argument:

| Project size | Post-delivery vs. design-stage fix cost |
| --- | --- |
| Large | ~100x |
| Small, non-critical | ~5:1 |

Same defect, same category of mistake. The multiplier isn't a property of
the bug — it's a property of how much finished work already sits on top
of it by the time someone else finds it.

[Read the paper →](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/962984/)

```notes
This is the citation that was missing from the earlier version of this
deck — the "survives" claim had no direct link. Boehm & Basili, "Software
Defect Reduction Top 10 List," IEEE Computer, Jan 2001.
```

## Where NIST's own number actually falls

NIST's 2002 report splits its $59.5B/$22.2B national estimate by who
absorbs it:

| Who bears it | Annual cost | Recoverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Software developers | $21.2B | $10.6B |
| Software users | $38.3B | $11.7B |

Nearly two-thirds of the cost, and most of what's actually recoverable,
sits on the far side of the sale — with the people who didn't write the
defect.

```notes
Same NIST Planning Report 02-3 already cited earlier — this is a second,
distinct table from the actual report body, not the headline aggregate.
Verified against the report's own extracted text, not secondary summaries.
```

## Two manufacturing sectors, in NIST's own figures

The same report breaks its estimate down by industry. Two are
manufacturing's own:

| Sector | Annual cost | Recoverable (50% error reduction) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Automotive | $1,229.7M | $377.0M |
| Aerospace | $237.4M | $54.5M |

This is the closest thing in the report to a manufacturing-specific dollar
figure — and it's still an aggregate across the whole sector, not a
per-defect price. It confirms the shape of the claim without letting us
launder it into a false precision.

## Case study: Therac-25

Between 1985 and 1987, a radiation therapy machine delivered massive
overdoses to six patients. Several died.

> "A detailed investigation of the factors involved in the
> software-related overdoses... presents lessons learned in terms of
> system engineering, software engineering, and government regulation of
> safety-critical systems."

**Leveson & Turner, *IEEE Computer*, 1993**

The race condition behind it existed in code carried over from an earlier,
less automated model — nobody re-verified it against the new machine's
removed hardware interlocks until after it had already shipped.

[Read the investigation →](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/274940)

```notes
This is the multiplier's ceiling — not "expensive," but irreversible.
Don't over-state the death count beyond what the paper itself says
("several") — that's the accurate, sourced figure.
```

## Case study: Ariane 5 Flight 501

37 seconds after liftoff on its maiden flight, the rocket veered off
course and was destroyed. ESA's own Inquiry Board, chaired by
Jacques-Louis Lions, found:

> "The failure... was due to specification and design errors in the
> software of the inertial reference system."

The software itself was inherited, unchanged, from Ariane 4 — where the
same code path had never been able to overflow. Nobody re-checked that
assumption against Ariane 5's faster trajectory.

> "The extensive reviews and tests carried out during the Ariane 5
> development programme did not include adequate analysis and testing."

[Read ESA's report →](https://www.esa.int/Newsroom/Press_Releases/Ariane_501_-_Presentation_of_Inquiry_Board_report)

```notes
The sharpest complication of the week: the last mile here wasn't
finishing new work, it was re-verifying old, already-trusted work carried
into a context where its own assumptions no longer held.
```

## Case study: Mars Climate Orbiter

The orbiter was lost approaching Mars in 1999. NASA's own Mishap
Investigation Board, chaired by Arthur Stephenson, found the root cause
was a units mismatch: one team's ground software produced thruster data
in pound-seconds, and the navigation team's software expected newton-
seconds, as the interface spec required.

Both pieces of software had presumably been tested on their own. Nobody
caught the mismatch until the whole system was already flying.

[Read NASA's Phase I report →](https://llis.nasa.gov/llis_lib/pdf/1009464main1_0641-mr.pdf)

```notes
Don't invent a dollar figure for this one — the primary report is a
scanned document with no reliably extractable cost figure, and other
sources disagree on exactly what's being totalled (spacecraft vs. launch
vs. whole program). Stick to the verified root cause.
```

## Case study: Boeing 737 MAX

Two crashes, five months apart, 346 people. The US House Committee on
Transportation and Infrastructure's 18-month investigation put it
bluntly:

> "The MAX crashes were... a horrific culmination of a series of faulty
> technical assumptions by Boeing's engineers, a lack of transparency on
> the part of Boeing's management, and grossly insufficient oversight by
> the FAA."

This is the organisational version of the same failure: MCAS, the flight
control software behind both crashes, existed inside a certification
process under competitive and schedule pressure to treat the last mile of
review as a formality rather than a gate.

[Read the Committee's report →](https://democrats-transportation.house.gov/imo/media/doc/final_boeing_737_max_report1.pdf)

```notes
This is the one case study here that isn't primarily a software-defect
story — it's what happens when the institution responsible for the
last-mile check is under the same pressure to finish as the team being
checked.
```

## The formula, made explicit

Quality engineers formalise this week's opening claim as an actual
equation, not just a category split:

**Cost of Quality = Cost of Conformance + Cost of Nonconformance**

Where conformance splits into **prevention** and **appraisal**, and
nonconformance splits into **internal failure** and **external failure**
— the same four buckets this week opened with.

> "Failure to identify a given cost as a quality cost, or incorrectly
> categorizing it, may result in misleading decisions on actions to
> improve quality."

**Mustafa Shraim, Ohio University — American Society for Engineering
Education, 2020**

[Read the paper →](https://peer.asee.org/a-simple-model-for-identifying-costs-of-quality.pdf)

```notes
This closes the loop with slide one's claim that manufacturing has "the
accounting other domains lack" — here's the actual accounting, not just
the assertion that one exists.
```

## The counter-example: designing the multiplier out

If catching a defect earlier is worth roughly a hundred times more than
catching it late, the rational response isn't just admitting that — it's
building a system that refuses to let a defect travel forward at all.

Toyota's own description of *jidoka*:

> "When an abnormality occurs... the machine or equipment can detect the
> abnormality and stop automatically, or the operator can stop the line
> by pulling the stop cord themselves... This eliminates the outflow of
> defective products."

[Read Toyota's own account →](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/index.html)

An andon cord is the cost curve, acted on: it forces every defect to be
caught at 1x, by making it organisationally impossible to let one reach
2x.

```notes
This is the thesis's own implication taken seriously, not a new example
of the same claim — if the earlier stages really are that much cheaper,
building a mechanism that refuses to skip them is the logical next
move, and it's one real manufacturers actually built.
```

## What four unrelated sources agree on

A peer-reviewed replication, a national economic-impact study, an
engineering-education paper, and four separate accident investigations —
different methods, different decades, different countries — all land on
the same structural claim:

- The multiplier is real, even where the famous chart wasn't.
- It falls hardest on whoever is downstream of the mistake.
- It compounds specifically because of what got built on top of the
  defect before anyone found it.
- The only design that reliably beats it is one that refuses to let a
  defect travel past the stage it was made in.

```notes
This slide has no new citation of its own — it's the synthesis of the
eight sources already cited across this run, and it's the bridge to the
closing slide's meta-point about verification.
```

# The chart needed its own last mile

**Manufacturing's version of "the last mile is expensive" looked
finished — a number, a source, decades of citations. It took someone
tracing the citation trail instead of trusting it before any of it could
actually be trusted.**

```notes
Land here, not on the 100x number. The real lesson of this week isn't
the multiplier, it's that even a peer-reviewed, replicated number still
needed its own verification pass before anyone should have leaned on it
— which is this course's argument, one level up.
```
