# 1x → 6.5x → 15x → 100x

**The chart everyone in software has seen at least once.**

---

## The claim

Cost to fix a defect, by the stage you catch it at:

| Stage | Multiplier |
| --- | --- |
| Design | 1x |
| Implementation | 6.5x |
| Testing | 15x |
| After release | 60–100x |

Usually credited to something called the **"IBM Systems Sciences Institute."**

```notes
Ask the room: who's seen this chart before, in a lecture or a blog post?
Most hands go up. That's the setup for the next slide.
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## The problem

> "The 'IBM Systems Sciences Institute' study has never been produced."

Software researchers Hillel Wayne and Laurent Bossavit traced the citation
trail back through decades of textbooks. It dead-ends in 1981 internal IBM
training notes, with no public dataset behind them.

A number that got laundered into "settled research" by being repeated —
not by being re-measured.

---

## What actually survives

**Boehm and Basili, 2001, peer-reviewed, *IEEE Computer*:**

Fixing a defect after delivery runs roughly **100x** more expensive than
catching it at requirements/design — on *large* projects. Their own paper
adds the caveat: closer to **5:1** on small, non-critical ones.

The order of magnitude held up under independent replication. The
impressive-sounding institute's name did not.

---

## Why this is a week 6 slide, not a footnote

A course built on finishing things properly can't cite a stat that never
finished being checked.

The multiplier in this week's slider is unit-less on purpose — every
dollar-denominated version we could find traces back to the same
unverifiable IBM lineage. The honest number is a ratio, not a price.
